Scan Cleaner

Erase students’ working and teachers’ red marks from a scanned worksheet — and keep the printed page pristine.

A marked-up worksheet has three kinds of ink on it. The printer toner that made the worksheet, the student’s pen that filled it in, and the teacher’s red pen that marked it. They all look dark to a scanner, so plain contrast tricks cannot tell them apart. This tool tells them apart by which colours of light each ink absorbs — a fingerprint that stays the same whether the stroke is a firm press or a faint scribble. Sample each ink once, and every stroke of that ink lifts off the page.

Scanned in black and white, so there is no colour to go on? Then the app switches to reading shape instead: printed type is machine-regular — every glyph the same height, every stroke the same width, every baseline exactly level — and handwriting is none of those things. How the separation works

Why darkness alone cannot separate the inks

A scanner reports how bright each pixel is. Printed toner, a blue ballpoint and a red marking pen are all dark, and a heavy pen press is darker than faint printed text. So any rule of the form “erase everything darker than X” either leaves the writing behind or takes the worksheet with it.

Colour helps, but naively: a faint blue stroke is a pale blue-grey, and a pale blue-grey sits numerically close to grey toner. The fix is to stop looking at the colour of the pixel and start looking at the colour of the ink.

The absorbance fingerprint

White paper reflects red, green and blue light equally. Ink subtracts from that. So for each pixel we measure how much of each channel went missing — its absorbance — and then ask only for the proportions:

A faint stroke absorbs less of everything — but in the same proportions. That is the whole trick: the fingerprint is unchanged by pen pressure, stroke thickness, scanner exposure or the shadow across the page. One sample of an ink therefore matches every stroke of it.

The rest of the pipeline
  • Flatten the paper. The paper’s own brightness is measured across a grid of the page and divided out, so shadows, a curled edge and yellowed paper all become plain white before anything is classified.
  • Classify every inked pixel against your samples by fingerprint distance, with printed toner given a protective head start.
  • Decide by stroke, not by pixel. Connected marks vote; a stroke that is 70% student ink goes entirely, faint edges included, so nothing dissolves into confetti.
  • Sweep the impurities. Specks below your despeckle size, scanner-lid borders and paper grain are wiped.
  • Restore the print. Surviving toner is pushed back to a solid black, and gaps left where a pen crossed a printed letter can be mended shut.
When the automatic pass struggles

If the student wrote in black ink, its fingerprint is genuinely the same as the printer’s — no amount of colour maths can separate them, and the app will say so under the samples. Use Zap stroke to click away whole handwritten marks, or the Erase brush for the rest. Pencil is usually slightly warmer than toner and often separates after two or three samples.

Key takeaway
Measure what an ink takes away from white light, not how dark it happens to look. Darkness changes with every stroke; the proportions do not.
